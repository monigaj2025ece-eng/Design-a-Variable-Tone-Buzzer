1. Introduction and prerequisites

A low-pass filter (LPF) is a circuit that passes signals below a selected frequency with relatively little attenuation and reduces signals above that frequency. An active low-pass filter uses an active device such as an operational amplifier (op-amp), usually with resistors and capacitors. The op-amp can provide voltage gain and buffer the filter from its load.

Core idea: a capacitor’s reactance magnitude is \(X_C=1/(2\pi fC)\). At low frequency it is large; at high frequency it is small. In the shown circuit, increasing frequency therefore shunts more signal toward ground at the RC node.

Learning objectives

  • Define passband, stopband, cutoff frequency, bandwidth, roll-off, and passband gain.
  • Derive the first-order non-inverting active LPF transfer function.
  • Calculate \(f_c\), gain in dB, magnitude, and phase.
  • Recognize ideal-op-amp assumptions and real-device limitations.

Prerequisites

Ohm’s law, impedance of R and C, complex frequency \(s=j\omega\), op-amp negative feedback, decibels, and basic Bode plots.

Key terminology

Passive RC low-pass, active first-order LPF, higher-order active LPF (Butterworth, Chebyshev, Bessel), and switched-capacitor/digital low-pass filters. This lesson focuses on a first-order active LPF.

3. Working principle

Step-by-step signal flow

  1. Low frequency: capacitor impedance is high, so the RC node is close to \(V_{in}\).
  2. Near cutoff: the capacitor increasingly diverts AC signal to ground; output magnitude is \(A_0/\sqrt2\) of passband gain.
  3. High frequency: capacitor impedance is low, so the RC-node voltage falls and the output is attenuated.
  4. Feedback: the op-amp attempts to keep its input difference small while operating linearly, giving the set closed-loop gain.

DC and AC behaviour

At DC: an ideal capacitor is open circuit, so the RC node follows the input; the output is the passband gain times the input, subject to offset and bias errors.

For AC: capacitor impedance decreases with frequency, creating a frequency-dependent divider and a pole at \(f_c\).

Phase: the RC pole adds lag \(-\tan^{-1}(f/f_c)\). At cutoff, phase lag is −45° for the first-order filter; the non-inverting gain stage adds no ideal phase inversion.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Ideal derivation assumes infinite op-amp input impedance, zero output impedance, unlimited bandwidth/slew rate, and linear operation.
  • One RC pole gives a gradual attenuation, not a brick-wall cutoff.
  • Op-amp finite gain-bandwidth can alter the response, especially when passband gain is high or cutoff approaches the op-amp’s usable bandwidth.
  • Large input amplitudes at high frequency can cause slew-rate distortion; output clipping occurs if the required output exceeds available swing.

Common misconceptions

  • Cutoff frequency is not the frequency where output becomes zero; it is the −3 dB point relative to passband.
  • At cutoff, voltage magnitude is about 70.7% of passband, not 50% (50% refers to power for the same impedance).
  • A first-order filter rolls off at −20 dB/decade only asymptotically above cutoff.
  • “Active” does not mean the filter always amplifies; gain depends on the op-amp feedback network.

4. Equations and derivation

Cutoff frequency

f_c = 1 / (2πRC) [Hz]

Here \(R\) is in ohms (Ω), \(C\) in farads (F), and \(f_c\) in hertz (Hz). This applies to a single-pole RC network with negligible loading.

Transfer function

The impedance of the capacitor is \(Z_C=1/(sC)\). By the voltage-divider rule,

H_RC(s) = V₁/Vin = ZC/(R + ZC) = 1/(1+sRC)

For a non-inverting op-amp gain stage \(A_0=1+R_f/R_g\), assuming the RC network is not significantly loaded:

H(s) = Vout/Vin = A₀/(1+sRC) = A₀/(1+s/ωc)

where \(\omega_c=1/RC=2\pi f_c\) in rad/s. Set \(s=j\omega\):

|H(jω)| = A₀ / √[1 + (f/fc)²]
∠H(jω) = −tan⁻¹(f/fc)
Gain(dB) = 20 log₁₀ |H(jω)|
At cutoff: \(f=f_c\), so \(|H|=A_0/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707A_0\). Relative to passband, \(20\log_{10}(1/\sqrt2)\approx-3.01\) dB and phase is −45°.

Useful limiting cases

ConditionApproximate responseMeaning
\(f \ll f_c\)\(|H|\approx A_0\), phase ≈ 0°Passband
\(f=f_c\)\(|H|=0.707A_0\), phase −45°Cutoff
\(f \gg f_c\)\(|H|\approx A_0 f_c/f\)−20 dB/decade slope

Design procedure

  1. Choose the desired cutoff \(f_c\) and passband gain \(A_0\).
  2. Select a practical capacitor value, then calculate \(R=1/(2\pi f_cC)\).
  3. Choose standard component values and recalculate the actual cutoff.
  4. Choose an op-amp whose bandwidth and slew rate comfortably exceed the signal requirements.
  5. Simulate AC sweep and transient response; compare against the equations.

5. Solved numerical problems

Example 1 — Calculate cutoff (Easy)

Given: \(R=10\,kΩ\), \(C=10\,nF\). Find \(f_c\).

Formula: \(f_c=1/(2\pi RC)\).

Substitute: \(RC=(10,000)(10×10^{-9})=100×10^{-6}s\).

\(f_c=1/(2\pi×100×10^{-6})≈1.59\,kHz\)

Interpretation: the output is about 0.707 of its passband value at approximately 1.59 kHz.

Example 2 — Find R and cutoff gain (Moderate)

Given: target \(f_c=2\,kHz\), \(C=22\,nF\), passband gain \(A_0=2\). Find R and output magnitude at cutoff for a 0.5 V peak input.

\(R=1/(2\pi f_c C)=1/(2\pi×2000×22×10^{-9})≈3.62\,kΩ\). Choose a nearby standard value such as 3.6 kΩ and recalculate actual cutoff.

At cutoff, \(V_{out,pk}=A_0V_{in,pk}/\sqrt2=2×0.5/1.414≈0.707\,V\).

Interpretation: cutoff means −3 dB relative to the 1.0 V peak passband output, not relative to the input.

Example 3 — Frequency response (GATE-style numerical)

Given: \(A_0=4\), \(f_c=1\,kHz\), \(f=3\,kHz\). Find magnitude and gain in dB.

\(|H|=4/\sqrt{1+(3/1)^2}=4/\sqrt{10}≈1.265\).

\(G_{dB}=20\log_{10}(1.265)≈2.04\,dB\).

Interpretation: output is still greater than input because passband gain is 4, although the filter attenuates relative to its own passband gain. Relative gain is \(1/\sqrt{10}=0.316\), or −10 dB.

Example 4 — Choose a capacitor (Moderate)

Given: \(R=4.7\,kΩ\), desired \(f_c≈1\,kHz\). Find C.

\(C=1/(2\pi Rf_c)=1/(2\pi×4700×1000)≈33.9\,nF\).

Choose 33 nF: \(f_c≈1/(2\pi×4700×33\,nF)≈1.03\,kHz\).

Shortcut: \(f_c\) is inversely proportional to \(R\) and \(C\). Doubling either one halves cutoff.

Suggested Multisim workflow

  1. Place an op-amp, R, C, feedback resistors, ground, and suitable power rails.
  2. Wire the RC node to the non-inverting input. Set feedback as a non-inverting amplifier.
  3. Use an AC source for AC analysis; set its small-signal amplitude consistently.
  4. Run AC sweep over a range such as \(0.1f_c\) to \(100f_c\), preferably on a logarithmic axis.
  5. Plot \(V(out)/V(in)\) magnitude in dB and phase. Identify the point 3 dB below passband gain.
  6. For transient analysis, test a sine wave below cutoff, at cutoff, and above cutoff with the same input amplitude.
  7. Check for clipping, unexpected phase, wrong supply pins, missing ground, and incorrect component connections.

8. Core electronics interview preparation

Expand each question to practise concise answers for analog design, validation, semiconductor, embedded, and electronics R&D roles.

1. What is a low-pass filter?

A frequency-selective circuit that passes lower frequencies and attenuates frequencies above its cutoff. It is used to reduce high-frequency noise and limit bandwidth.

2. Why is cutoff called the −3 dB point?

At cutoff, voltage magnitude is \(1/\sqrt2\) of the passband magnitude. Since \(20\log_{10}(1/\sqrt2)≈−3.01\) dB, it is the half-power point for equal impedances.

3. How do you calculate cutoff for a first-order RC stage?

Use \(f_c=1/(2πRC)\), with R in ohms, C in farads, and the result in hertz. This assumes a single RC pole and negligible loading.

4. What is the roll-off of a first-order LPF?

Approximately −20 dB/decade or −6 dB/octave well above cutoff. Each additional pole contributes another −20 dB/decade asymptotically.

5. What happens to phase at cutoff?

The first-order RC pole contributes −45° at cutoff. At very low frequency phase approaches 0° and at very high frequency it approaches −90°.

6. Why use an active filter instead of a passive RC filter?

An active filter can provide gain and buffering, reducing load interaction. It needs power and is limited by op-amp bandwidth, noise, output swing, and slew rate.

7. Your simulated cutoff differs from theory. What do you check?

Verify actual R/C values and units, AC source settings, sweep scale, op-amp model and rails, loading, feedback wiring, and whether the −3 dB reference is measured from passband gain.

8. Why can a real op-amp distort at high frequency?

Finite gain-bandwidth reduces closed-loop gain; limited slew rate limits the maximum output slope; output swing and input common-mode range can also be exceeded.

9. How would you test the filter on a bench?

Apply a known sine wave, sweep frequency, measure input/output amplitudes and phase, compute \(20\log_{10}(V_o/V_i)\), and compare cutoff and roll-off with theory. Avoid clipping and use proper probe grounding.

10. How does a second-order LPF differ?

It has two poles and typically rolls off at −40 dB/decade far above cutoff. Its damping/Q affects passband shape and possible peaking.

11. What is the effect of doubling R while C stays fixed?

Cutoff halves because \(f_c∝1/R\). The change can also affect resistor thermal noise and interaction with source/load impedances.

12. Where is this used in industry?

Sensor signal conditioning, anti-aliasing before ADCs, audio tone shaping, reference/noise filtering, and measurement front ends. Requirements determine whether a simple RC or a higher-order precision filter is needed.

Industry perspective

In a sensor front end, a low-pass filter can suppress out-of-band electrical noise before an ADC. Engineers balance cutoff, signal bandwidth, settling time, noise, component tolerance, ADC acquisition behaviour, and anti-aliasing requirements. A first-order filter is simple, but may not attenuate sufficiently when a sharp anti-aliasing transition is required.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages

  • Simple and low cost.
  • Active version can provide gain and buffering.
  • Useful for noise reduction and bandwidth limiting.

Limitations

  • First-order attenuation is gradual.
  • Active circuit needs power.
  • Op-amp noise, offset, GBW, slew rate, and output swing affect performance.
  • R/C tolerances shift cutoff.

High-priority exam points

  • Memorize the −3 dB definition and the \(1/\sqrt2\) amplitude ratio.
  • Use hertz for \(f_c\), radians/second for \(\omega_c\), and convert nF/µF to farads.
  • One pole: −20 dB/decade; phase transitions from 0° to −90°.
  • For non-inverting gain, \(A_0=1+R_f/R_g\), assuming standard negative feedback.
  • Do not confuse absolute gain in dB with attenuation relative to passband gain.

Troubleshooting checklist

  • No output: check supply rails, ground, op-amp pinout, input signal, and output connection.
  • Output clips: reduce input amplitude or gain; verify supply and output swing.
  • Wrong cutoff: verify R/C units and measure −3 dB relative to passband.
  • Unexpected oscillation: inspect feedback wiring, decoupling, load capacitance, and op-amp stability.
  • Different measured phase: check probe/reference polarity and simulation plot conventions.
